Headrest rods: two Ø8 carbon rods, cut to 85 + H mm

Every mast has two solid carbon rods running its full length. They stand in the chair's socket and reach up to just under the pad. The rods roughly double the mast's strength. The five lengths form one even series, 85, 110, 135, 160 and 185 mm, and one 500 mm rod gives two full pairs.

Why the rods matter, and why full length

A push on the pad bends the mast like a lever. The bending is biggest at the socket exit (yellow) and shrinks to almost nothing under the pad. So the rods' main job is to carry the socket exit.

Answer: full length is not stronger at the weak point, but it is never worse. It is a little stiffer (H75), and it leaves no rod tip inside the stem, where a sudden stiffness change would make a small stress point. So the rods stay full length.

Holding the rod: printed gauge with a clamp, or a small bar clamp

Option 1, printed (designed, in the print list): a block with a V-groove for the rod, an end stop, and a saw slot at each length. A printed lid near the stop is pulled down by 2× M3 screws and nuts (already in the order) and grips the rod. Screw the flanges to a scrap board, or hold the block flat. Push the rod against the stop, tighten the lid, cut through the slot for your height. You get the length, a square cut, and your hand stays away from the disc. Files: models/seating/headrest/stl/tools/rod_cutting_gauge_base.stl (2.2 h, 70 g) and …_lid.stl (0.25 h, 7 g), PETG, 3 walls, 15 %, no supports.

Option 2, a small bar clamp (any hardware store): a 6-inch (150 mm) one-handed ratchet clamp holds the gauge or a piece of wood on the table, or the rod itself (wrap it in tape so the jaws don't crush the fibres). It's useful for other jobs too, but on its own a round rod can roll, so it works best together with the gauge or a V-groove.

Use both: the clamp holds the gauge on the table, and the gauge holds the rod. That's the safest setup once you start cutting carbon regularly.

Sealing the cut ends

A cut end has loose fibres. Seal it with a drop of thin CA (superglue) on the end and about 5 mm along the side. It soaks in, hardens, and adds no thickness; sand lightly and add a second drop. A heat-shrink sleeve is also a common way, but it adds 0.5–1 mm and no longer fits the 8.3 mm hole; it would need the hole widened to about 9 mm (a model change). Once the rod is in the mast it is fully enclosed in plastic, so the dust risk is only while cutting and handling.

On hold: steel parts that need no cutting

Some standard machine parts are Ø8 steel rods sold in fixed lengths. They would mean no cutting and no dust, but they are heavier, and each needs a check (and some a model change) before use. Nothing here is adopted. It's for you to understand the options.

1. Hardened dowel pin

A plain, solid, precision-ground steel pin. Machines use it to line up two parts exactly. It's hardened all the way through.

Lengths
Ø8 only up to 80 mm, so it fits H0 only
Strength
very hard, but brittle (snaps, doesn't bend)

2. Mould ejector pin

The pin that pushes a finished plastic part out of an injection mould: a long ground rod with a thin round head. Made of tool steel with a hardened skin, and sold in fixed lengths.

Lengths
100 / 125 / 160 mm (none at 80)
Strength
close to carbon (~1400 MPa), and it bends before it breaks
Change
a printed pocket for the head at the bottom of the plug

3. Linear shaft (3D-printer rod)

The smooth shiny rods a 3D printer's carriage slides on. Only a thin outer skin (yellow) is hardened; the core is ordinary steel.

Lengths
100 / 150 / 200 mm, about $3–4 each
Strength
weakest of these: roughly 35–60 % of carbon

4. M8 socket head screw, class 12.9

An ordinary bolt with a round head and a hex key socket, in the strongest common grade (12.9, marked on the head). The smooth shank carries the bending. The thread is thinner, so it goes at the top end, where the load is low.

Lengths
every 10 mm; sold in any Israeli fastener shop, ₪2–5
Strength
yield ~1100 MPa, bends before it breaks
Change
head pocket in the plug; thread section still to check. Not zinc-plated, not stainless.

If the plastic cracks, the two rods hold the load alone. What matters then is how much stress each material takes before it breaks or stays bent. The right column is the safety factor of the tallest mast (H100) with the rods alone.

Ruled out

  • PVC rod: about as strong as the PETG around it, so it adds nothing. Carbon is ~30× stronger.
  • Threaded rod or ordinary bolts: the thread makes the rod thinner, and the common grades are too weak.
  • Aluminium: bends and stays bent at a quarter of the needed load.
  • Tent poles, arrow shafts, kite spars: long thin tubes, so you still cut them, and they're weaker.
  • Hex keys, screwdriver extensions: one length only, or too thin.
  • Rods from old printers: free, but every one still needs a grinder to cut.

What happens now

Build with carbon: one 8×500 rod, cut to 85 + H using the plans above. The headrest README and print guide say the same.

Cut with the printed gauge (in the print list, print it before the rods are cut), held on the table with a small clamp. Seal both ends with thin CA.
